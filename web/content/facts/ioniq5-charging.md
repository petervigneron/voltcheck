# Hyundai Ioniq 5 (2022–2025 model years verified): Charging

## DC fast charging: 2022–2024 (CCS1 port)

- 2022 (77.4 kWh pack, the only pack in Hyundai's 2022 spec sheet): 10%→80% on a 150 kW (400V)-class charger takes 25 minutes (Hyundai labels this figure "est."). 10%→80% on a >250 kW (800V)-class charger takes 18 minutes.[^1]
- 2023 and 2024 (both spec sheets show identical figures). RWD Standard Range (58.0 kWh): 10%→80% on a 150 kW (400V)-class charger is not specified by Hyundai ("TBD"). 10%→80% on a >250 kW (800V)-class charger takes 18 minutes. RWD and AWD (77.4 kWh): 10%→80% on a 150 kW (400V)-class charger takes 25 minutes ("est."). 10%→80% on a >250 kW (800V)-class charger takes 18 minutes.[^2]
- Hyundai's 2022 Owner's Manual gives a different charger-power breakdown for the same generation. On a 350 kW charger, 10%→80% takes about 18 minutes on both the Standard and Extended battery. On a 50 kW charger, 10%→80% takes about 63 minutes (Standard) or 73 minutes (Extended). The manual's AC charge time and pack lineup conflict with the 2022 spec sheet. The manual gives an AC charge time of 5 hours 50 minutes for the Standard battery and 7 hours 10 minutes for the Extended battery. The 2022 spec sheet gives one 77.4 kWh pack with an AC charge time of 6 hours 43 minutes. The manual's Standard/Extended split does not match the 2022 spec sheet's single-pack lineup; Standard Range was not introduced until MY2023. The manual's file metadata carries a ModDate of March 17, 2023, twelve months after its original creation date. This sheet does not resolve the conflict.[^3]

## DC fast charging: 2025 (native NACS port)

- RWD Standard Range (63.0 kWh): 10%→80% via the native NACS port on a 150 kW (400V)-class charger takes 24 minutes. 10%→80% via the included CCS adapter on a 50 kW charger takes 1 hour 1 minute. 10%→80% via the CCS adapter on a >250 kW (800V)-class charger takes 20 minutes.[^4]
- RWD and AWD (84.0 kWh): 10%→80% via the native NACS port on a 150 kW (400V)-class charger takes 30 minutes. 10%→80% via the CCS adapter on a 50 kW charger takes 1 hour 21 minutes. 10%→80% via the CCS adapter on a >250 kW (800V)-class charger takes 20 minutes.[^4]
- The 2025 Ioniq 5's native NACS port currently charges at up to 125 kW at a Tesla Supercharger station. Hyundai's own wording: "125kW at this time."[^5]
- That 125 kW Supercharger cap is lower than the 150 kW (400V)-class rate the spec sheet's NACS charge-time figures above are built on. Both numbers come from Hyundai directly. This sheet does not reconcile them further.
- On the 2025 Ioniq 5, reaching an 800V-class charger 10%→80% in 20 minutes (both packs) requires the included CCS adapter and a CCS1 station. Going through the native NACS port to a Tesla Supercharger is slower: 24 or 30 minutes to 80%, and further capped at 125 kW.
- Per Hyundai's Supercharger-generation compatibility table, the NACS-port 2025 Ioniq 5 is not compatible with V1 (100 kW) or V2 (125/150 kW) Supercharger stalls. It is compatible with V3 (250 kW) and V4 (350 kW, "currently 250 kW") stalls.[^6]

## AC (Level 2, home) charging

- Onboard AC charger: 10.9 kW, standard on every US Ioniq 5 trim, every model year 2022 through 2025.[^7]
- Full charge (10%→100%) on AC Level II, 240V. 2022 (77.4 kWh pack, the only one in that year's sheet): 6 hours 43 minutes. 2023 and 2024: 5 hours 50 minutes (58.0 kWh Standard Range) or 7 hours 10 minutes (77.4 kWh Long Range). 2025: 5 hours 40 minutes (63.0 kWh Standard Range) or 7 hours 20 minutes (84.0 kWh Long Range).[^8]

## Connector by model year

- Model years 2022 through 2024 ship with a native Combined Charging System (CCS) port, not NACS. Hyundai's own words describe these as "CCS-port-equipped Hyundai EV drivers," and list "Model-year 2024 and earlier ... IONIQ 5" among the eligible models.[^9]
- The performance 2025 IONIQ 5 N also has a CCS port, not NACS. Both press releases below separately list "2025 IONIQ 5 N" among the models eligible for a CCS-to-NACS adapter, an adapter only a CCS-port vehicle would need.[^10]
- The 2025 Ioniq 5 (excluding the N performance model) is a native NACS vehicle. Hyundai's own words: "IONIQ 5 aims to further enhance customer satisfaction by being the first Hyundai model to come equipped with the Tesla North American Charging Standard (NACS) port."[^11]
- Separately and independently, Hyundai's owner-resources FAQ states the same fact: "25MY IONIQ 5 will have a native NACS port."[^12]
- The Ioniq 5 N does not get the native NACS port for 2025. Hyundai's own words: "NOTE: IONIQ 5 N vehicles do not have a native NACS port."[^13]

### Adapter direction 1: 2022–2024 CCS1-port Ioniq 5 to a Tesla Supercharger

- Requires a Hyundai-issued CCS-to-NACS adapter. Hyundai's own words: this adapter "is essential for reliable charging at V3 Tesla Superchargers and other NACS DC fast charging stations."[^14]
- Free of charge to owners/lessees who purchased or leased their vehicle on or before January 31, 2025, claimed through the MyHyundai account.[^14]
- For anyone who bought after January 31, 2025: MSRP is $250, and the dealer sets the actual selling price. Hyundai's own words: "Even though MSRP is $250, the dealer's selling price and final cost of the NACS adapter is completely up to the dealer as they have 100% discretion."[^15]
- Using a non-Hyundai adapter is not described as prohibited, but Hyundai's warranty will not cover resulting damage. Hyundai's own words: "HMA warranty will not cover damage caused by non-Hyundai accessories. Only use adapters with CSA or UL Certification."[^16]

### Adapter direction 2: 2025 native-NACS Ioniq 5 to a CCS1 station

- Comes standard with a CCS adapter at delivery. Hyundai's own words: "Does the 25MY IONIQ 5 customer have to order the CCS adapter themselves? No, it will be included with the 25MY IONIQ 5."[^17]
- The CCS adapter's purpose, in Hyundai's own words: "This adapter give[s] access to charging networks such as Electrify America and EVgo that currently only support the CCS standard."[^18]
- Hyundai's own owner's manual confirms the reverse framing directly. The 2025 manual documents "Using the CCS1 DC Charging Adapter," described as usable "if the DC charger installed at the public charging station is a CCS1 DC charger." That phrasing implies the vehicle's own port is not CCS1.[^19]
- If the CCS adapter is missing at delivery, Hyundai directs the owner to their dealer for a free replacement.[^20]

## Preconditioning by model year

- 2022: the only automatic battery-warming feature is the "High Voltage Battery Warmer System." It activates automatically once the charging connector is plugged in. It does not activate proactively while driving toward a charger. Hyundai's own words: "If the charging connector is connected, the warmer system automatically operates according to the battery temperature."[^21]
- 2022 also has a separate, manual-only toggle called "Winter Mode," selected or deselected in EV Settings, which raises battery temperature ahead of driving/DC charging in cold weather. Nothing in the 2022 manual ties this mode to a navigation destination.[^22]
- 2023: the same manual toggle is renamed "Battery conditioning mode (if equipped)," with identical select/deselect operation in EV Settings and the same cold-weather framing. The 2023 manual does not contain a navigation-linked activation path for this mode.[^23]
- 2024: "Battery conditioning mode" is still a manual EV Settings toggle, but the manual adds a navigation-linked behavior for the first time. Hyundai's own words: "If you set the DC charging station as your destination while using the battery conditioning mode, the battery temperature is optimized for charging, and charging time may be maintained to the level being charged in room temperature."[^24]
- 2025: the feature (now called "Battery Conditioning") gets two independent activation paths. Hyundai's own words: "You can manually operate the Battery Conditioning function or automatically operate the function by setting a DC charging station as a destination or a waypoint." Manual operation is Home screen, then Electric Vehicle, then the settings icon, then Battery Conditioning, then Active. Automatic operation triggers by setting a DC charging station as the nav destination or a waypoint, without the driver first turning the manual toggle on.[^25]
- 2022, 2023, and 2025: the manual states the mode is restricted to vehicles equipped with the battery heater. The 2022 and 2023 manuals: "This mode is available for the vehicles equipped with the battery heater." The 2025 manual: "Battery Conditioning function operates only in a vehicle equipped with a battery heater."[^22][^23][^25]
- 2024: the section heading carries an "if equipped" badge, but the 2024 manual's Battery conditioning mode section does not itself say what the vehicle must be equipped with. It never uses the words "battery heater" anywhere in that section (checked against the full manual text, not just this section). The restriction almost certainly still means the battery heater, matching every adjacent model year, but that inference is not sourced to the 2024 manual's own words and is not asserted as a quote here.[^24]
- The battery heater is not standard on every pack in 2023 and 2024. Per the 2023 and 2024 spec sheets, "Battery preconditioning function" is standard ("S") on the RWD and AWD (Long Range) pack, and not marked standard on the RWD Standard Range pack. The 2022 spec sheet, before the Standard Range trim appears in Hyundai's documents, splits this by drivetrain instead: standard on AWD, not marked standard on RWD. The 2025 spec sheet marks it standard on both packs: the battery-heating row is a single merged "S" cell spanning the RWD Standard Range and RWD/AWD columns. Every 2025 Ioniq 5, including RWD Standard Range, gets the battery heater standard.[^8]

## Battery packs

- 2022 (as documented in Hyundai's 2022 spec sheet): a single 77.4 kWh pack, 697V, RWD and AWD.[^8]
- 2023 and 2024: RWD Standard Range at 58.0 kWh, 522.7V. RWD and AWD (Long Range) at 77.4 kWh, 697V.[^8]
- 2025 (refreshed): RWD Standard Range grew to 63.0 kWh, 523V. RWD and AWD (Long Range) grew to 84.0 kWh, 697V.[^8]

## Scope note

Verified against Hyundai's own spec sheets and owner's manuals for model years 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025 (non-N Ioniq 5) only. A standalone hyundainews.com spec PDF for the 2026 non-N Ioniq 5 could not be located. The only 2026 IONIQ 5 spec PDF found on hyundainews.com is for the IONIQ 5 N performance model, which is out of scope here. The N does not get the native NACS port even in 2025/2026, per its own cited FAQ note. Nothing in this sheet should be read as applying to model year 2026 without a separate check against a 2026-specific source. The Ioniq 5 N throughout is out of scope: its charging hardware, connector, and preconditioning behavior were not independently checked and may differ from the standard Ioniq 5 covered above.

The 2024 and 2025 owner's manuals were fetched from a mirror (cdn.dealereprocess.org) after the same file path pattern that worked for the 2022 and 2023 manuals returned HTTP 404 on owners.hyundaiusa.com for those two model years. Both mirrored files carry visible Hyundai branding on their cover pages and match Hyundai's own naming convention.

The tension between the 2025 spec sheet's NACS charge-time figures (built on a 150 kW/400V charger class) and the NACS Adapter FAQ's "125kW at this time" Supercharger cap is reported as-is. Both are Hyundai's own material. This sheet does not attempt to resolve which one describes real-world Supercharger sessions more precisely.
